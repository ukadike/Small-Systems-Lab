African Sovereign Compute

Energy. Materials. Computation. Intelligence.

Canonical research branch · 17 September 2026

Thesis

African AI sovereignty cannot stop at data sovereignty or local-language models. It requires meaningful control across the full computational chain.

ENERGY → MINERALS → MATERIALS → COMPONENTS → CHIPS → COMPUTE → DATA → MODELS → APPLICATIONS → GOVERNANCE
What would an African-controlled computational stack look like if energy, materials, hardware architecture, models, data and social purpose were designed together rather than inherited from a hyperscale model built elsewhere?

This program does not assume that Africa must immediately reproduce a leading-edge GPU fabrication ecosystem. It investigates a staged route toward control of more of the stack: materials research, design IP, low-power accelerators, packaging and testing, distributed compute, energy systems, model development and public-interest deployment.

Why this research exists

The African Union's Continental AI Strategy calls for an Africa-owned, people-centered, development-oriented and inclusive AI ecosystem, including infrastructure, talent, datasets, innovation and safeguards. The International Energy Agency reports that Africa supplies major shares of several critical minerals while capturing less than 1% of the value generated from manufacturing clean-energy technologies and components. USGS reports that the Democratic Republic of the Congo accounted for an estimated 75% of global cobalt output and 51% of global tantalum output in 2024.

The AEN question is therefore not simply whether minerals exist on the continent. It is where knowledge, processing, design, manufacturing, intellectual property, computation and economic value accumulate.

Research ladder

1. AEN Edge Compute

Design extremely energy-efficient AI/ML nodes that can operate with intermittent grids, local renewable generation and limited cloud dependence.

2. African chip architecture and design

Investigate African-owned accelerator architectures, open instruction sets, RTL, compiler/toolchain development, domain-specific processors and licensing strategies. Early generations may use external foundries while retaining African design ownership and IP.

3. Advanced packaging, testing and electronics manufacturing

Map realistic entry points into semiconductor assembly, packaging, board production, validation, repair and testing.

4. Semiconductor-material beneficiation

Study the path from extracted minerals to semiconductor-grade and electronics-grade materials, including refining, chemical processing, purity, metrology and environmental safeguards.

5. Power electronics

Develop components and control systems optimized for AEN solar, storage, microgrid and distributed-compute environments.

6. Thermal architecture

Research low-water and water-independent cooling, heat reuse, passive thermal systems and thermal storage for edge and regional compute.

7. Regional fabrication capability

Treat fabrication as a long-term industrial capability whose scale and process node should be selected around African use cases, markets, workforce and energy realities rather than prestige alone.

First prototype

The first practical target is an AEN-powered African edge-compute node: solar-first, storage-aware, capable of local inference, designed for graceful operation under unstable grid conditions, repairable, accessible and governed transparently.

Compute Sovereignty

Small Systems Lab uses Compute Sovereignty as the shared frame connecting AEN and Omoluabi.

ENERGY + MATERIALS + HARDWARE + COMPUTE + DATA + MODELS + GOVERNANCE

Possessing raw minerals alone is not sovereignty. Possessing datasets while renting all compute is not sovereignty. Running local-language models on infrastructure whose price, access or terms are externally controlled is incomplete sovereignty.

Relationship to Omoluabi

AEN investigates how independent computational infrastructure can physically exist. Omoluabi's Civic AI Governance branch investigates who controls that infrastructure, who benefits from it, how decisions are made, what rights communities retain and what accountability mechanisms should exist.

AEN builds the physical possibility. Omoluabi investigates the civic conditions.

Research questions

  • Which parts of the semiconductor value chain are realistic early African entry points?
  • Which minerals relevant to compute are mined in Africa, where are they refined, and where does economic value accumulate?
  • Can open architectures reduce dependence on proprietary accelerator ecosystems?
  • What processor designs perform well under energy and thermal constraints?
  • Can distributed edge systems substitute for some hyperscale workloads?
  • How should heat recovery, storage and low-water cooling be measured?
  • What should qualify as African ownership: geography, capital, IP, workforce, governance, or combinations?
  • How do we avoid reproducing extractive labor or environmental practices inside Africa?

Research principle

This program does not assume a single “African way of thinking.” Africa contains diverse languages, knowledge systems, economies, political structures and technical traditions. The objective is African agency, plurality and the capacity to choose and build, not another technological universalism.
